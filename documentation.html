Documentation

When I first started on this project, I knew I wanted to work on something that felt both personal and relatable. I’ve always been curious about how people stay active and how workout habits change as they age. So, when I came across a large fitness dataset on Kaggle, I instantly knew it was the one I wanted to use. The dataset contained information from about 20,000 participants, including age, height, workout type, exercise name, calories burned every 30 minutes, etc. My goal was to present the dataset through visualization and tell a story about how people exercise at different stages of life.

Initially, I wanted to create a visualization that shows the relationship between diet habits, age group, and exercise type. But I realized there wasn’t enough diet-related information to make that idea work. The dataset had more information on exercise habits, so I decided to just focus on workout patterns by age group.

From there, I started sketching ideas on paper. I wanted the visualization to feel simple and fun to interact with, something you could understand at first glance. I knew I wanted to build it as a one-page layout, where everything happens in one place. The top section would be the main chart, and the bottom section would display detailed results after a user clicks on an age group. I wanted an interface that was clean, organized, and easy to navigate.

When it came to color choices, I tried a few combinations. I eventually went with blue as the main color and yellow as the highlight color. Blue felt like the right choice because it’s calming and works great against a white background. It naturally draws the viewer’s eyes toward the chart without taking too much attention. I then picked the yellow highlight color to create a contrast. It’s bright, energetic, and instantly noticeable. Together, they make the interface both functional and visually engaging. When I got the main graph working, I started on the detailed information. The reader can click on any age group bar and see the most performed exercises. That part was both fun and challenging to build because it felt like it brought the whole thing to life. I placed the detailed exercise table right below the bar chart so the reader wouldn’t have to scroll around or switch pages.

While playing with the dataset, I discovered a few patterns. People in their 20s tend to do high-intensity, strength-based workouts like flutter kicks, lunges, thrusters, dips, and glute bridges. On the other hand, people in their 50s liked exercises like scissors kicks, deadlifts, Turkish getups, squats, and planks - activities that focus more on core stability, balance, and posture. Seeing this pattern across the age groups was really interesting because it showed how fitness goals change with age, from building strength to maintaining flexibility and stability. This project showed me how design and data can work together to make information meaningful. As someone pursuing a data-related career in healthcare, I can see how we can help uncover meaningful patterns. We can use data in a way that helps people understand themselves and the world a little better. I also learned how small design choices like color, layout, and interactivity can change how someone experiences data.
